I. Introduction

Background of the Study

After seven years of working night shift at a call center, I experienced troubling health changes including hair thinning, elevated cortisol levels, and weakened immunity. This personal experience mirrors a growing trend in today's labor force. The global demand for night shift workers has increased because of the rise of 24-hour economies driven by globalization, digital connectivity, and the expansion of industries such as the Business Process Outsourcing (BPO), healthcare, and information technology (IT). Many businesses and corporations began operating across multiple time zones which increased the need for several employees to work during the night (Lartey, 2024).

A significant number of these night shift occupations are sedentary in nature with excessive screen time and minimal physical activity. This combination can pose serious health risks. One of which is chronodisruption caused by misalignments of the circadian rhythm due to irregular sleep-wake cycles (Vetter et al., 2016). Prolonged exposure to artificial light and digital screens at night may also aggravate these effects by disrupting hormonal regulation and reducing sleep quality (Silvani et al., 2022).

Various research studies suggest different approaches to tackle the problem of night shift work. These include caffeine restrictions, naps, and short breaks for recovery, however, the interventions given are often too broad and do not give clear indications about the optimal timing within the night shift for each intervention. Furthermore, night-shift employees are generally put under conditions of quick decision-making under fatigue and therefore are not able to apply consistently the circadian rules to their daily schedules. This highly indicates the necessity of having tools that convert circadian science into practical, real-time guidance.

With these considerations in mind, this study proposes the development of an interactive, circadian-aware planner application designed to support night-shift workers in timing caffeine intake, naps, and micro-care practices. Moreover, the study seeks to bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical, day-to-day decision-making by synthesizing existing scientific literature into a timing-based, interactive planning framework.

Statement of the Problem

The health risks associated to night-shift work and sedentary activity have been thoroughly studied and documented in research reports, however, those same reports often do not consider the suggested interventions of caffeine regulation, napping, and recovery practices as connected strategies instead of being coextensive behaviors controlled by timing of the circadian rhythm. There is a lack of production on the best timing of these interventions during the different phases of a night shift. Furthermore, few studies examine the use of interactive or planning-based digital tools that translate circadian science into practical decision support for night-shift workers operating in multimedia-intensive environments.

Objectives of the Study

The objective of this study is to collect scientific knowledge to guide the development of a user-friendly and circadian-aware planner for night shift workers. Specifically, it aims to:

  1. Identify physical, mental, and cognitive health risks among sedentary workers on prolonged night shifts;
  2. Study evidence-based interventions related to caffeine use, napping strategies, and micro-care interventions for night-shift workers;
  3. Investigate the interaction between circadian timing and the optimal timing of these interventions across different stages of the night shift; and
  4. Translate synthesized findings into a conceptual framework and design logic for an interactive planner application that supports healthier timing-based decision-making.

Significance of the Study

The significance of this research flows aligns with a public health problem that has hardly been addressed in the existing literature: the dual health hazards posed by sedentary work and night-shift occupations. And with industries such as BPO, healthcare, and IT actively propagating around-the-clock operations, a comprehensive understanding of the long-term effects on workers becomes vital.

This study fills an important gap at the intersection of occupational health, circadian science, and multimedia system design. By considering the implementation of timing-based interventions as its main approach, this study offers a practical angle to the literature rather than just documenting health risks.

The findings of this study will be beneficial to different groups. Healthcare providers may use the findings for formulating preventive care programs and targeted health education for shift workers such as behavioral timing strategies. Employers and HR employees may derive findings on creating healthier environments. Authorities may use the research in informing their guidelines on occupational health. The study also offers designers and developers evidence-informed foundation that can support the development of circadian-aware digital tools within health-related application design. Finally, this will bring awareness to affected workers of the dangers and possible interventions to safeguard their long-term health.

Scope and Limitations of the Study

This study is limited to the review and synthesis of peer-reviewed literature related to sedentary night-shift work, circadian disruption, caffeine use, napping strategies, and micro-care interventions in multimedia-intensive work environments. The primary output of the study is an interactive, circadian-aware planner application developed as a research-informed prototype.

II. Study Framework

Health Risks

Night shift work has been extensively associated with adverse physical health outcomes. Research consistently indicates higher risks of metabolic syndrome, diabetes, obesity, and hypertension among night shift workers. An umbrella review conducted by Kervezee et al. (2022) found that night shift workers exhibited a 10% increase in diabetes risk, 25–38% greater likelihood of obesity, and approximately 30% higher risk of developing hypertension compared to day shift workers. These conditions largely result from disrupted melatonin secretion and increased stress hormones due to nighttime exposure to artificial lighting and prolonged wakefulness.

The psychological implications of sedentary night shift work are substantial. Shift Work Sleep Disorder (SWSD), affecting nearly 40% of night shift workers, is strongly associated with elevated levels of anxiety, depression, and chronic fatigue (Boivin & Boudreau, 2014). Additionally, night shift routines contribute to social isolation and increased stress, exacerbating psychological distress and mental health issues over prolonged periods. The cognitive effects of night shift work are equally concerning. Wickwire et al. (2021) highlighted in their systematic review that cognitive performance notably declines with each consecutive night shift, emphasizing impairments in attention, working memory, and response inhibition. Long-term night shift workers have also demonstrated persistent executive-function deficits, raising concerns about potential increased risks for dementia and accelerated cognitive aging.

Comparison with Day Shift Office Workers

Comparative studies have consistently shown distinct health disparities between sedentary night shift and day shift office workers. Night shift employees, due to disrupted circadian rhythms and increased sedentary behavior, report higher cortisol levels and poorer immune function compared to day shift counterparts, who generally exhibit better hormonal balance and improved immune markers (Kervezee et al., 2022).

Furthermore, day shift workers typically benefit from more stable sleep patterns, fewer sleep disturbances, and greater overall life satisfaction, reinforcing the protective effects of circadian alignment.

Caffeine Use and Circadian Timing in Night-Shift Work

Caffeine has been reported to be one of the most effective methods for managing fatigue among night-shift workers, mainly because of its ability to temporarily block sleep and increase alertness. This is the main reason why caffeine is so popular among workers who have to stay awake for long hours at night. Its regular use has also been proven to help maintain cognitive abilities and lessen the severity of fatigue, which is a crucial aspect in occupational group where safety and screen usage is the major concern (McHill et al., 2014).

Though caffeine share some benefits with the sleep, its effect on the body is completely opposite, as it does not correct the down-mapped body cycle or reset the biological clock. Circadian science asserts that caffeine has a stimulating effect and is not a circadian regulator and hence cannot compensate for the difficulty of changes in the body clock (chronodisruption) that come with night-shift work. Those working night shifts are usually guided to suit their caffeine intake accordingly and employ strategies, such as taking small doses early during the shift while not taking any close to when they plan to sleep, in order to reduce disruption of their sleep (Lockley, n.d.).

Furthermore, the experimental evidence indicates that caffeine could directly act on the circadian clock. Burke et al. (2015) showed that evening caffeine intake could cause melatonin to be secreted later, thus having a phase-delaying effect on the circadian rhythm. This implies that wrong-timed caffeine consumption could worsen circadian disruption by postponing the biological night, hence reducing post-shift sleep quality. Additionally, other researchers have claimed that caffeine taken during circadian misalignment can lead to longer time to sleep, less deep sleep, and worse sleep structure even if working at night (McHill et al., 2014).

When all the studies are considered together, it can be said that caffeine is a good short-term alertness booster for night-shift workers if used correctly, but its advantages are strongly based on the timing concerning the circadian phase and sleep intention. Yet, the majority of the studies give general recommendations rather than specific timing guidance that corresponds to different parts of a night shift. This deficiency emphasizes the importance of having circadian-aware planning tools that convert scientific knowledge about caffeine timing into everyday practical decision support for night-shift workers.

Napping Strategies and Sleep Inertia Among Night-Shift Workers

Napping has been widely explored as a countermeasure to fatigue and sleepiness in night-shift work, particularly among professions with extended work hours such as nursing. Planned nap strategies are considered effective in mitigating nocturnal sleepiness and performance deficits, despite the transient effect of sleep inertia immediately following awakening (Ruggiero & Redeker, 2013). A systematic review of experimental and quasi-experimental studies found that night-shift napping often reduces subjective sleepiness and improves sleep-related performance during shift hours, although the magnitude of benefit varies according to nap timing and duration (Ruggiero & Redeker, 2013).

An implementation project among hospital nurses demonstrated that when organizational and managerial barriers to napping were reduced, napping during night shifts was feasible and associated with reduced workplace sleepiness and less drowsy driving after work. In this study, nurses reported that most naps resulted in light or deep sleep, and sleep inertia was infrequent, suggesting that naps of moderate duration can be integrated into night-shift schedules without significantly compromising immediate post-nap performance (Geiger-Brown et al., 2016).

Experimental research on simulated night work further clarifies the relationship between nap timing, duration, and sleep inertia. In controlled laboratory settings, night workers who took two distinct naps during extended night work experienced notable improvements in fatigue and performance in the early morning, despite evidence of sleep inertia immediately after awakening. Performance measures such as vigilance and cognitive testing were superior in nap conditions compared to no-nap conditions, and naps scheduled closer to the early morning period appeared particularly effective in sustaining alertness later in the shift (Oriyama & Miyakoshi, 2018).

Most importantly, the research indicates that sleep inertia—grogginess and disorientation upon awakening—was experienced immediately after naps but eventually reduced, showing that controlling for duration and timing may minimize sleeping inertia's negative effects (Oriyama & Miyakoshi, 2018).

In a comparable experiment, researchers found out that the time chosen for naps—night or early morning—will determine how the sleeping state and performance will be maintained. They also argued that not all nap timings have the same benefits and that careful scheduling in relation to circadian lows boosts the overall effectiveness which is an important insight for creating interactive planning tools aimed at night-shift workers (Oriyama & Miyakoshi, 2018).

All these studies together highlight the intricate nature of napping strategies in night-shift scenarios. Although naps provide benefits in alertness and performance, the issue of sleep inertia which momentarily hinders cognitive function after awakening exists, hence the need for careful consideration of nap timing and duration to derive maximum benefits. The literatures advocate the necessity of evidence-based, circadian-aware recommendations instead of universal napping tips, particularly when incorporated into tools like interactive planner applications that are intended to help night-shift workers manage their schedules and alertness throughout their shifts.

Micro-Care Interventions: Breathing, Movement, and Short Recovery Practices

Micro-care interventions are understood as the brief, structured activities which are mainly meant to promote the body's physiological recovery, lessen fatigue, and ultimately enhance the performance of functions during long tiring work interruptions. These interventions are of great significance to night-shift laborers as their bodies get out of sync with the day-night cycle, they have to undergo heavy mental tasks, and they are sedentary for long periods.

Studies in the healthcare sector and among shift workers have shown that short recovery practices, commonly known as microbreaks, are capable of lessening the cumulative effects of fatigue and at the same time not causing workflow interruptions. A systematic review by Dall'Ora et al. (2020) assessed the impact of fatigue and recovery on healthcare workers and pointed out that very short rest breaks such as stretching and controlled breathing were linked to livening up one's alertness and energy perception, and even to improving one's performance. The authors stress that such interventions are particularly effective in the case of shift work, where long breaks are not a possibility (Dall'Ora et al., 2020).

Similarly, Tucker (2018) elaborated on the issue of short breaks being one of the means of tackling occupational fatigue and he pointed out that a minute's movement and posture change in the course of a work shift are enough to get the better of musculoskeletal strain and lesser attention. The review brought out the point that even if the movement is of low intensity, still it can in a way act as a regulator for the body and thus can prevent and mellow down the effects that sitting for long periods, often the case among night-shift workers, can bring about.

Although there is a mounting body of evidence that supports micro-care practices, the current research barely describes the timing of these interventions during the different phases of a night shift or their coordination with other fatigue-management strategies like caffeine consumption and napping. The absence of timing-specific guidance restricts the practical use of micro-care strategies and emphasizes the necessity for circadian-aware planning tools which are capable of interpreting evidence into practical, phase-based recommendations for night-shift workers.

Theoretical Framework

Examining the health effects of sedentary night shift work requires the use of theories, as they are essential in providing a structured lens for understanding the mechanisms underlying its impact on the body and mind. Theories explain the answers as to how disruptions in natural circadian rhythms, combined with occupational and lifestyle factors, can result to long-term adverse health effects. With this, the study gains both scientific depth and a coherent foundation for interpretation of findings. This framework is anchored in multidisciplinary perspectives that encompass occupational psychology, biology, sedentary behavior research, and environmental health. Together, these perspectives establish a comprehensive theoretical basis that connects night shift work, sedentary lifestyle, and health outcomes in a unified framework.

The Demand-Control Model (DCM) developed by Karasek emphasizes from the perspective of occupational psychology that employees who are under high job demands but have little control over their tasks are more likely to suffer adverse health outcomes in some form, such as job strain, fatigue, or psychosomatic illness (de Jonge et al., 2000). Sedentary night shift work often puts an employee under high cognitive and performance demands, such as constant monitoring or a repetitive routine in clerical tasks, whereas limited autonomy reflects high demands from both task and environment. An imbalanced demand-control relationship tends to amplify the stress response, which later evolves into either psychological or physical health problems.

The Circadian Rhythm Theory, from the biological perspective, explains how night shift work disrupts the internal biological clock of the body. Circadian rhythms regulate important physiological happenings such as sleep-wake cycles, hormone secretion, and metabolic activities. Night work functions against these natural rhythms, causing what is referred to as chronodisruption, which has been robustly correlated with an increased risk of sleep disorders, obesity, cardiovascular diseases, and diabetes (Baron & Reid, 2015). By disrupting the synchronization between internal biological timing and external work schedules, sedentary night shift work impairs recovery from work and long-term health.

Moreover, another critical outlook is the Sedentary Work Hypothesis, which emphasizes the hazards posed by prolonged inactivity. Prolonged sitting, a characteristic feature of night shifts such as the call center operations or monitoring jobs, has been linked to metabolic dysfunctions, cardiovascular diseases, and musculoskeletal stresses (Owen et al., 2010). Unlike the physical occupations, sedentary night shift workers bear the risks of inactivity on a background of disrupted circadian rhythms that further amplify the negative effects.

Lastly, the Artificial Light Theory describes how exposure to artificial light at night intensifies health risks. As cited in the study of Cho et al. (2015), it was revealed that bright or blue-spectrum light at night inhibits melatonin secretion, delays sleep onset, and shifts circadian rhythms, leading to degradation in the quality of sleep and enhanced physiological stress. Prolonged used of monitors and exposure to artificial lighting contribute to the nocturnal lifestyle of night shift workers by making them susceptible to insomnia, chronic fatigue, and metabolic imbalance, thereby making artificial light a major environmental disease factor against their health status.

Effectiveness in visual communication

Comment 3: why a course study guideCourse materials are legitimate sources when they are the actual origin of the framework being applied. Goli-Cruz (2023b) identifies five factors that determine whether a visual communication material succeeds with its intended audience: relevance, clarity, visual appeal, inclusivity, and language. Relevance is described in terms of perceived personal consequence, since "we tend to give more attention to messages/information that we think can impact our lives," while clarity is constrained by the medium, because "there is limited time (for video) and space (for static visual material)," so that "the message should be concise and direct to the point" (Goli-Cruz, 2023b).

Three of these factors carry direct consequences for a night-shift planner. Relevance implies that a plan which does not visibly respond to the user's own reported state will be read as generic advice and disregarded. Clarity implies that guidance competing with an active work task must be short enough to absorb during a break rather than studied. Inclusivity, defined as attention to "the diverse needs, perspectives, experiences, and characteristics of the audience" (Goli-Cruz, 2023b), extends beyond accessibility compliance to the recognition that night-shift populations vary in shift length, break autonomy, and commute mode, a variation this study addresses through user-adjustable planning parameters rather than a single fixed schedule.

Content architecture

Comment 9: a real paper, not a handoutDeRose et al. (1990) is peer-reviewed and thirty-five years old, which is exactly why it is useful: it makes the content and presentation separation an established principle rather than a contemporary web-development convention. It is also the only source in the folder that speaks to data structure. Note: the available copy is a reprint with its own pagination; journal pages are 3–26.

Because the planner delivers guidance in several forms (timed text instruction, a guided breathing animation, timeline entries, and summary readings derived from logged events), and because further forms are anticipated, the representation of that content is itself a design question. DeRose et al. (1990) argue that a document is properly modeled as "an ordered hierarchy of content objects," in which each object carries a semantic type rather than formatting instructions, since an object's "appearance is superficial and transient rather than essential." They observe that systems storing appearance rather than structure cannot answer natural questions about their own content, "because there is no explicit indication of what part of the text is poetry," and that a hierarchical content model can "integrate a wide variety of different types of data or media into a 'compound document'" (DeRose et al., 1990).

This study adopts that model for its plan items. Each item is stored as a structured object carrying its identifier, scheduled time, intervention category, instruction, and rationale, with visual treatment resolved at presentation time from the category rather than embedded in the item. The consequence is that one item definition can be rendered as a timeline entry, a reminder, or an exported record without duplication, and that adding a further rendering, such as an audio reading of an instruction or a video of a movement sequence, is a change to presentation rather than to stored content. That property is what makes the multimodal commitment stated in Chapter I structurally available to the prototype even where a given modality is not yet built.

The reflection screen

Comment 13: why two logging interfaces is a finding, not a redundancyA reviewer seeing thirteen event types after Table 1 promises five will read it as an inconsistency. Framed as two interfaces answering different questions under different conditions, the same fact becomes evidence that the interruption cost of in-shift logging was designed around rather than ignored.

The prototype separates logging into two interfaces on the grounds that they answer different questions under different conditions. During a shift the user is interrupted and the question is what is happening now, so the in-shift interface is reduced to the five events that alter the remainder of the plan, each returning a single sentence of consequence. After the shift the user is not interrupted and the question is what happened, so the reflection screen exposes the full set of thirteen event types, allows a time to be stated or corrected, and permits any entry to be amended or removed. Clarity, in Goli-Cruz's (2023b) sense of a message constrained by the time and space available to receive it, is therefore a property of the moment rather than of the application, and the two interfaces implement two different answers to it.

The screen returns two derived readings rather than a bare list of entries: a summary of the night's pattern read from the logs, and an explicit statement of which plan items changed as a consequence of them. This is the relevance factor applied to feedback rather than to content. A user who cannot see that a logged event altered the plan has no evidence that logging is worth the effort, and the study treats that visibility as a functional requirement rather than as reassurance.

A closing set of seven fixed-choice questions records the user's own account of the night: sleep duration, restedness, the period of greatest sleepiness, the effect of caffeine, completion of movement breaks, whether rest was taken, and what the plan should change next shift. Only the last of these is permitted to alter the stored profile. The remainder inform the following plan without overwriting the user's stated characteristics, on the reasoning that an answer about what the plan should do next is a preference, while an answer about how rested the user feels is an observation of one night; treating the second as the first would allow a single bad shift to rewrite a standing profile. The design principle at work is the one stated in Chapter I under scope, that the planner adjusts timing rather than diagnosing the user, expressed as a constraint on which inputs are allowed to write.

III. Methodology

Research Design

The study will use a qualitative, design-oriented research design to assist in the review of scientific literature and the development of a circadian-aware interactive planner for night-shift workers. Qualitative research is characterized by its focus on understanding complex phenomena through looking into the context, processes, and relationships of concepts rather than performing statistical tests.

Findings on circadian rhythms, caffeine use, napping, and micro-care practices will be synthesized to derive timing-based principles, which inform the planner's decision rules and design logic. Validation by the experts will ensure both the soundness of the concept and alignment with current evidence of the developed interactive planner.

This method encourages theory-driven design and practical innovation, as it is based on the existing knowledge rather than the collection of new data while consequently keeping the methodological coherence with the objectives of the study (Domínguez, 2017).

The prototype was developed under the Prototype Model of the software development life cycle. This model "focuses on developing a prototype version of the project" and "is applicable if the customer does not have yet specific details about the software they want to have," proceeding by presenting an early build for feedback and applying "necessary changes… based on clients' comments and suggestions"; it is characterized as iterative and as adopting "a trial-and-error method between the client and the developer" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023a).

Comment 15: justify by exclusionNaming the chosen model is weak on its own. Naming the rejected model and quoting its stated disadvantages shows the choice was made rather than defaulted. Two sentences, noticeably more credible.

The model was selected on the grounds the same source gives for model selection generally: "finding the right one greatly depends on the circumstances and factors including but not limited to the scope of the project, size of the team, budget, and client preferences" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023a). Three circumstances of this study favor the Prototype Model. First, its requirements were derived from literature rather than elicited from a client, so no fixed specification existed at the outset. Second, it was developed by a single researcher, for whom the coordination overhead that justifies heavier models does not arise. Third, the Waterfall Model was rejected because its documented disadvantages, that "output is delivered late, severe errors were not detected early" and that it "does not support iterative development" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023a), are precisely the risks a literature-derived prototype must avoid, since a timing rule misread from a study should surface during construction rather than after it.

The same iterative commitment is independently supported from the design side, where graphic design is described as "an iterative process" in which "before a graphic design is approved, produced, and used, revisions after revisions may happen," and where proceeding directly to production is discouraged "so as not to waste human and non-human resources" (Goli-Cruz, 2023b).

Research Methodology

This study will employ a qualitative, design-oriented research methodology grounded in the systematic review and synthesis of existing scientific literature. The methodology is structured to translate established circadian and occupational health research into the design of an interactive, circadian-aware planner application for night-shift workers. Rather than evaluating health outcomes or user effectiveness, the study will focus on evidence integration, design logic formulation, and expert validation.

Literature review as the evidence base

The study will begin with a systematic review of scientific articles and literatures which have been peer-reviewed and are about the topics of circadian rhythms and night-shift work, caffeine timing, napping strategies, fatigue management, and micro-care interventions like breathing and mobility practices. Academic databases will be searched for, and relevant studies will be screened according to their connection with sedentary night-shift workers and timing-based behavioral recommendations.

From the selected literature, actionable timing principles will be extracted, specifically addressing what intervention should be applied, when it should occur, and under what conditions it is most appropriate. These findings are summarized and organized into a literature matrix structured according to different phases of the night shift, namely pre-shift, mid-shift, and post-shift periods.

Translation of evidence into application rules and features

The findings obtained from the synthesis will be interpreted as decision logic that will be easily integrated into an interactive planner. The scientifically backed recommendations will take the form of if-then rules that will lead the way for suggestions dependent on time. To illustrate, the inputs based on sleep duration, shift start time, and reported levels of fatigue will be utilized to produce the recommendations for napping, caffeine restriction, and micro-care practices that are appropriate for the given time.

The design of application functionalities like daily check-ins, reminder notifications, structured schedules, and suggested routines will be guided by the decision rules. This stage will guarantee that there is a direct link between scientific evidence and the application's capabilities.

Design of interaction and multimodal content

Based on the derived design logic, the interaction structure of the planner application will be conceptualized. Core user flows will include onboarding procedures that collect shift schedules and user goals, daily check-ins that capture sleep, energy levels, and caffeine use, and planner outputs that present personalized timing plans.

Comment 17: the specification and the build, kept apartThe paragraph above is the specification and is worth keeping as written. The one below states which parts of it the assessed build actually delivers. Splitting the two is what lets Chapter II argue for audio and video as design directions without the methodology reading as a description of features that do not exist.

To support varied user needs and reduce cognitive load, multimodal content will be incorporated, including text-based guidance, audio-based breathing exercises, short instructional videos for movement or stretching, and simple visual representations such as timelines or charts. Of these, the build assessed by this study implements text-based guidance, timed visual instruction, and the visual representations, namely the timeline, the day strip, and the summary readings. The audio and video variants are specified here and in Chapter II as design directions, and are not present in the assessed build.

Prototype development

A functional minimum viable prototype (MVP) will be developed to demonstrate the operationalization of the synthesized evidence. The prototype includes a shift-based scheduling engine, a rule-based recommendation system, a notification and reminder component, and a basic content player for multimodal micro-care materials. Logging functionality will be included to record recommended actions and completed activities. The scope of the prototype will be intentionally limited to essential features and three primary shift phases to maintain methodological focus.

Comment 18: the largest gap between specification and buildReminder delivery is the one absent component a panelist is most likely to raise unprompted, because timing is the study's whole premise. The prototype has the labels and the schedule; what it does not have is the ability to interrupt. Declared here in one sentence rather than defended later. Closing it means notification scheduling, which pulls in permissions and a service worker — scope decision, not a small fix.

Four of these five components are present in the assessed build: the scheduling engine, the rule-based recommendation system, the content player, and logging, the last extended with correction of entries and an export of the stored record. The notification and reminder component is not. The prototype composes and displays the reminder schedule but has no means of delivering an alert to a user who is not looking at it, so the component is recorded as specified and unbuilt. The three shift phases of the literature matrix are resolved by the scheduling engine into six named intervals, namely pre-shift, early shift, mid-shift, late shift, post-shift, and sleep, since a recommendation whose evidence distinguishes the start of a shift from its final hours cannot be placed against a single mid-shift period.

Software Design

Comment 21: a quotable justification for module count"Why four modules and not one, or twelve?" is a fair question with no obvious answer. This quote gives one: complexity is minimized across modules and their integration, not within modules alone.

Design proceeded across the four aspects identified in the reference framework, where design is understood as the stage that "link[s] the requirement analysis phase to the actual system implementation" and where, if requirements analysis "answers the question 'what', software design answers 'how'" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023c). Data design specifies the plan item structure described in Chapter II. Architectural design partitions the system into four modules (time arithmetic, planning rules, statistics, and presentation) on the principle that "overall complexity is the sum of the complexity of each module plus the complexity of the actual integration," and that "complexity represents cost" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023c).

Procedural design is expressed as the rule set that transforms a user profile and event log into a timed plan. The rule set exhibits both patterns the framework describes: it is transform-centered, in that "the input data is then transformed into an output data," and transaction-centered, in that "the input data is evaluated and alternative paths will be selected based on the result of the evaluation" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023c). The latter accounts for the conditional adjustments through which a logged skipped break, failed nap, or late caffeine dose alters subsequent recommendations. Interface design is treated in Chapter II.

Accessibility as a Design Requirement

Accessibility is treated in this study as a functional requirement rather than an optional enhancement, following the position that "it is your responsibility to ensure that the digital products you produce are accessible to people of all abilities" (Esteves, 2025). The conventional justification for accessible design rests on permanent disability. This study advances an additional justification specific to its population.

Comment 22: the strongest argument availableThis connects Chapter II's own cited evidence (Wickwire) to a discipline the thesis had not yet used. It reframes accessibility from a compliance chapter into a restatement of the study's central problem, which is both true and unusually persuasive. If one paragraph from this integration survives, it should be this one.

Night-shift workers exhibit measurable declines in attention, working memory, and response inhibition that accumulate across consecutive shifts (Wickwire et al., 2021). An interface operated under those conditions faces constraints functionally similar to those that accessibility standards are constructed to accommodate, differing in origin, situational rather than permanent, but not in kind. Conformance to WCAG 2.2 at Level AA (World Wide Web Consortium [W3C], 2023) is therefore adopted as a design requirement responding directly to the cognitive profile documented in Chapter II, and not as an ancillary compliance activity.

Comment 23: the vocabulary the previous paragraph is missingThe situational argument is strong but leaves an opening: "so you are calling tiredness a disability?" This paragraph closes it by borrowing the course's own three-way distinction, which lets the claim be made about design rather than about the user's status.

The course from which this requirement is drawn distinguishes universal design, inclusive design, and accessible design, and treats meeting the needs of users whose capacities vary, including ageing users, as continuous with meeting the needs of users with disabilities (Esteves, 2025). That continuity is what allows the preceding argument to be made without overstatement. The study does not claim that fatigue constitutes a disability; it claims that a design accommodating varied and fluctuating capacity serves both populations, and that the techniques are the same in either case.

Comment 24: naming the procedure, not just the standardCiting WCAG alone says which rules were used. Naming the preliminary review, the tooling and the WCAG-EM report format says the audit was conducted a particular way, which is the difference between a procedure a reader can repeat and a claim a reader must accept. The WCAG-EM report tool output belongs in an appendix once the audit is re-run on the finished build.

Conformance is organized by the four principles under which the success criteria are grouped: perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust (W3C, 2023). The procedure follows the sequence taught for accessibility auditing, namely a preliminary review of the obvious barriers, followed by a structured conformance evaluation conducted under the Website Accessibility Conformance Evaluation Methodology and recorded in its report format (Esteves, 2025; W3C, 2014). The evaluation is restricted to the prototype's implemented screens, and its scope statement records that restriction explicitly, since a conformance claim covering screens that do not yet exist would be void.

Verification and Validation

Comment 28: the single most useful correctionThe original Chapter III used "validation" for everything. Splitting the terms costs one paragraph and reorganizes the whole section: the test suite and traceability check become verification, the expert review becomes validation. Both were already being done; only the vocabulary was missing.

To preserve the archival nature of the study, assessment is conducted without collecting primary data from end users. Within that constraint, this study distinguishes verification from validation following Boehm, where verification asks "are we building the product right?" and validation asks "are we building the right product?", the latter being necessary because "there are times when the specification requirements do not represent the real needs of the customers" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023d). The distinction reorganizes the study's assessment activities into two groups.

Comment 29: the traceability promise, made operationalChapter III already promised a traceability check. Storing citation keys on each plan item turns that promise from an appendix maintained by hand into something the system can report on. It is also the clearest evidence that the circadian literature genuinely governs the artifact. The check covers generated plan items. The five micro-care activities are recommendations that carry no keys yet — either widen the check or keep the scoping sentence.

Verification comprises two procedures. Automated unit tests exercise the scheduling, statistics, and time-arithmetic modules, together with storage, focus management, and the color tokens, unit testing being the strategy that "focuses on checking if the unit of code (smallest testable part) is fit to use" and is "done by the developer itself" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023d). The traceability check confirms that each planning rule corresponds to at least one supporting study, and is performed against citation identifiers recorded on each generated plan item alongside its user-facing rationale, so that the check is executed against the running system rather than against separately maintained documentation. Rules resting on design judgment rather than on evidence carry an explicit marker to that effect, so that the absence of a citation is a recorded state rather than an omission.

Comment 30: legitimizing the method most open to challengeExpert review instead of user testing is the study's most contestable choice. This citation establishes heuristic evaluation as taught disciplinary practice, making it a deliberate selection rather than a substitute for testing that could not be arranged.

Validation comprises expert heuristic review and scenario-based testing. Heuristic evaluation is adopted as an established interface assessment method in multimedia practice, taught alongside usability testing as a means to "evaluate a user interface using heuristics and usability tests" and to "create user interfaces based on heuristic and human-centered principles" (Clariño, 2024). Its use here is a methodological selection appropriate to a prototype whose scope excludes end-user trials, and is not offered as equivalent to them. Scenario-based testing uses hypothetical user cases to confirm that the planner's outputs are consistent with the synthesized timing rules.

Comment 31: an instrument with provenanceChapter III requires a "structured evaluation guide." Adapting a rubric that already exists and citing it is stronger than composing one at submission time, and it takes an afternoon rather than a week.

The evaluation instrument adapts the criteria of an established design-analysis rubric, namely context and purpose, enumeration of visual elements and design principles applied or violated, assessment of effectiveness against intended purpose, and improvement recommendations (Goli-Cruz, 2023c). Numerical scoring is removed in favor of qualitative judgment, consistent with the instrument requirements stated below.

Respondents of the Study

The study will not involve any respondent in the traditional sense. No surveys, interviews, experiments, or direct participation from night-shift workers will be conducted. Instead, subject-matter experts in relevant fields such as sleep science, occupational health, digital health design, or human-computer interaction are consulted solely for the purpose of expert validation.

The role of these experts is limited to evaluating the conceptual framework, design logic, and alignment of application features with existing scientific evidence. Their input is used to assess the credibility and coherence of the proposed prototype rather than to generate empirical data.

Data Gathering Procedure

In the process of gathering data, internet searches will be conducted from academic databases. Selection criteria include relevance to night-shift work, circadian disruption, fatigue management, caffeine timing, napping strategies, micro-care interventions, and sedentary or multimedia-intensive work environments.

Once selected, studies are examined to extract key findings related to behavioral timing, contextual conditions, and recommended interventions. Extracted information is organized according to intervention type and phase of the night shift, namely pre-shift, mid-shift, and post-shift. This organization facilitates comparative analysis and supports the synthesis of timing-based principles.

Research Instrument

The primary research instruments that will be used in this study are literature extraction matrices and document analysis that will be developed by the researcher. These tools are used in systematically recording key variables, timing, recommendations, contextual constraints, and intervention outcomes reported in the literature.

For the validation of the prototype, a structured evaluation guide will be utilized to assess the conceptual soundness, usability logic, and scientific compatibility of the proposed application features. The instrument will be focused on qualitative judgements rather than numerical scoring and will be designed to ensure traceability between scientific evidence and design decisions.

Data Analysis

The data analysis comprises thematic synthesizing whereby the findings of relevant literature are coded and categorized according to reoccurring themes. These include the associations of sedentary work with circadian disruption and screen exposure and interventions which attempt to mitigate the health effects associated. Comparative insights between daytime and nighttime sedentary workers are further integrated according to study objectives. The analysis focuses on congruence and divergence within and across disciplines such as biology, psychology, and occupational health. Emphasis is placed on identifying timing-based principles that can be operationalized within night-shift schedules.

Synthesized findings are translated into decision rules and design logic that inform the structure and functionality of the interactive planner application. The analysis does not involve statistical testing but relies on interpretive reasoning and expert judgment to ensure conceptual coherence and evidence alignment.

Limitations of the Assessment Approach

Comment 32: the limitation that also justifies the testsThis quote does two jobs: it bounds the claims that can be made, and it explains why a student prototype has an automated test suite at all. Health-adjacent software warrants a higher confidence level; that is the argument, and it comes from the source.

Two limitations follow from the approach above. First, testing establishes the presence of defects but not their absence: it "discloses the presence of bugs, but not their absence," so that "we can only address the problems that we have encountered but we cannot do something about the ones we did not discover" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023d). The point carries additional weight for this study, since "systems concerning health should have a higher level of confidence than that of supply inventory" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023d), and a prototype validated by expert review alone therefore retains residual risk that the study does not claim to eliminate.

Second, acceptance testing, the strategy that verifies "if the requirements of the clients are all satisfied" and is "usually carried out by the end-users" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023d), falls outside the declared scope of this study. Accordingly, no claim is advanced regarding the prototype's effectiveness in improving health outcomes or altering behavior, consistent with the scope and limitations set out in Chapter I.

Ethical Considerations

This study adheres to the ethical standards that apply to research and it relies solely on available, peer-reviewed literature, and expert consultation. Proper citation and attribution of the sources cited will ensure plagiarism is avoided and academic integrity is upheld.

Expert validation will be done through informed consent and each participant will be notified about the extent and the reason of their participation. During the whole research process, no personal, sensitive, or identifiable data will be collected. Moreover, since the study will not treat human beings as respondents or experimental participants, the risks concerning privacy, harm, or coercion are extremely low.
