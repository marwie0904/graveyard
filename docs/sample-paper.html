II. Study Framework

Design Foundations of the Planner Interface

[Preceding sections establish the health risks of sedentary night-shift work, the evidence base for caffeine timing, napping strategies, and micro-care interventions, and the four theories anchoring the study.]

Comment 1: the hingeThis sentence is the bridge between the circadian half of the thesis and the design half. Everything before it justifies what the app says; everything after justifies how. Without this paragraph a panelist can fairly ask why a Multimedia Studies thesis spends four chapters on sleep science. Source: MMS 149 Module 4, "Software Design Aspects."

The literature reviewed thus far establishes what a circadian-aware planner should recommend and when it should recommend it. It does not establish how those recommendations should be presented. This distinction matters because the intervention proposed by this study is not a set of guidelines but an artifact that a fatigued worker must operate at three o'clock in the morning; the evidence base determines the content of the recommendation, while design determines whether the recommendation is received at all. Software engineering literature states the point plainly: "a highly-efficient system might still not be accepted if the interface is rejected by the users" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023c). Accordingly, this section reviews the design principles that govern how timing-based guidance is rendered into an interface.

Effectiveness in visual communication

Comment 2: why a course study guideCourse materials are legitimate sources when they are the actual origin of the framework being applied. The five-factor model is used here as an organizing structure for design decisions, so citing the guide is more honest than citing a general design textbook the study did not use. Flagged: confirm whether the panel accepts unpublished course materials in the reference list.

Goli-Cruz (2023b) identifies five factors that determine whether a visual communication material succeeds with its intended audience: relevance, clarity, visual appeal, inclusivity, and language. Relevance is described in terms of perceived personal consequence, since "we tend to give more attention to messages/information that we think can impact our lives," while clarity is constrained by the medium, because "there is limited time (for video) and space (for static visual material)," so that "the message should be concise and direct to the point" (Goli-Cruz, 2023b).

Three of these factors carry direct consequences for a night-shift planner. Relevance implies that a plan which does not visibly respond to the user's own reported state will be read as generic advice and disregarded. Clarity implies that guidance competing with an active work task must be short enough to absorb during a break rather than studied. Inclusivity, defined as attention to "the diverse needs, perspectives, experiences, and characteristics of the audience" (Goli-Cruz, 2023b), extends beyond accessibility compliance to the recognition that night-shift populations vary in shift length, break autonomy, and commute mode, a variation this study addresses through user-adjustable planning parameters rather than a single fixed schedule.

Comment 3: naming what already existsThe prototype's timeline was built before this vocabulary was applied to it. Using the terms retroactively is not dishonest; it is documentation. What would be dishonest is claiming the principles generated the design. The prose deliberately says the principles "carry the weight of" the view rather than "produced" it.

The elements and principles of design supply the vocabulary for the resulting layout. Goli-Cruz (2023a) identifies the elements as "color, typography, line, space, shape, texture, and depth" and the principles as "balance, contrast, emphasis, hierarchy, movement, pattern, proportion, repetition, and unity," noting that the principles "provide a framework for organizing and arranging the elements in a harmonious and pleasing composition." Three principles carry the weight of the planner's timeline view: hierarchy, which distinguishes an item's title from its rationale; contrast, which separates one intervention domain from another; and proximity, which groups items belonging to the same phase of the shift.

Comment 4: pre-empting a questionA panel will ask whether a Western-derived color-and-icon vocabulary suits a Filipino user base. Raising it first, and routing it to expert validation, converts an unanswerable objection into a stated limitation. Cheaper than defending the choice.

Goli-Cruz (2023a) further observes that "visual codes are important aspects of visual design as they can convey meanings that are culturally bound," and that "knowing your audience is an important consideration when developing your graphic design." Since the target population of this study is composed largely of Filipino business process outsourcing workers, the study treats color semantics and iconography as culturally situated rather than universal, and identifies this as a matter for expert review rather than one settled by the researcher.

Aesthetic grounding

Comment 5: the strongest aesthetic citationHeinrich is peer-reviewed (Evolutionary Psychology, 2013), unlike the course guides. More importantly he ties aesthetics to well-being, which is this thesis's actual subject. That single link is why MMS 170 belongs here at all: it turns "the app looks calm" into an argument continuous with the circadian chapters.

The decision to render the interface as calm, low-contrast, and visually restrained rather than gamified requires justification beyond preference. Heinrich (2013) argues that aesthetic response is an evolved adaptation to environment rather than an arbitrary cultural overlay, concluding that "if aesthetic tastes refer to adaptive responses to living in a certain environment, then this suggests that we should make efforts to retain and create environments that we find beautiful and pleasing because our well-being depends on it" (p. 752). This study extends that claim to the digital environment: for a worker whose physical setting is already misaligned with their biology, an interface that adds visual noise compounds the misalignment, while one that reduces it performs a small corrective function.

Comment 6: a citation used defensivelyReviewers commonly suggest making a health app "more engaging." This quote is held in reserve for that feedback: restraint is defended as a design position with a source behind it, not as an absence of effort.

Heinrich (2013) also notes that "the aesthetic environment includes not only our visual cues but also an aural landscape" (p. 752). This observation is recorded as the basis for an audio variant of the planner's breathing guidance, on grounds independent of the cognitive-load argument advanced in Chapter III. The build assessed by this study delivers that guidance visually and by timed interval rather than by sound, so the passage is retained here as a stated design direction and not as a description of the implemented prototype. On novelty, Heinrich cautions that although "the 'new' catches our eye and so variety and complexity as such may be aesthetic," there are "discrete limits to the novel that are beautiful," since "making something ugly is not innovation" (p. 755), a constraint this study reads as an argument against decorative elaboration in an interface used under fatigue.

Comment 7: citing a source against yourselfDutton supplies the palette argument and then undercuts how far it can be pushed. Including both halves is deliberate: a panel that spots the limitation unaided will treat the whole aesthetic section as naïve. Stating it first converts a weakness into evidence of critical reading.

Dutton (2005) reports cross-cultural evidence bearing on palette selection, noting that in a ten-country survey of art preferences "blue turned out to be the favourite colour worldwide, with green in second place" (p. 698), alongside consistent preferences for water, vegetation, and open landscape. The planner's color assignments for hydration and movement follow this pattern. Dutton is equally clear about the limits of such reasoning, however, distinguishing Kant's category of the agreeable, "the straightforward subjective sensations of things that we like in direct experience: the taste of sweet, for example, or the colour blue," from the beautiful, and observing that a theory of inherited landscape preference offers little help in explaining "what distinguishes a popular calendar landscape from a great landscape painting" (p. 703). Evolutionary accounts are therefore used in this study to justify broad choices such as palette and visual calm, and not to adjudicate finer compositional decisions, which were settled instead by the design principles above and by expert review.

Content architecture

Comment 8: a real paper, not a handoutDeRose et al. (1990) is peer-reviewed and thirty-five years old, which is exactly why it is useful: it makes the content and presentation separation an established principle rather than a contemporary web-development convention. It is also the only source in the folder that speaks to data structure. Note: the available copy is a reprint with its own pagination; journal pages are 3–26.

Because the planner delivers guidance in several forms (timed text instruction, a guided breathing animation, timeline entries, and summary readings derived from logged events), and because further forms are anticipated, the representation of that content is itself a design question. DeRose et al. (1990) argue that a document is properly modeled as "an ordered hierarchy of content objects," in which each object carries a semantic type rather than formatting instructions, since an object's "appearance is superficial and transient rather than essential." They observe that systems storing appearance rather than structure cannot answer natural questions about their own content, "because there is no explicit indication of what part of the text is poetry," and that a hierarchical content model can "integrate a wide variety of different types of data or media into a 'compound document'" (DeRose et al., 1990).

This study adopts that model for its plan items. Each item is stored as a structured object carrying its identifier, scheduled time, intervention category, instruction, and rationale, with visual treatment resolved at presentation time from the category rather than embedded in the item. The consequence is that one item definition can be rendered as a timeline entry, a reminder, or an exported record without duplication, and that adding a further rendering, such as an audio reading of an instruction or a video of a movement sequence, is a change to presentation rather than to stored content. That property is what makes the multimodal commitment stated in Chapter I structurally available to the prototype even where a given modality is not yet built.

Application to the Prototype's Support Screens

Two of the prototype's four screens deliver something other than the plan itself: one presents the micro-care activities, the other returns the shift to the user afterwards. They are treated separately here because the questions they raise, namely how a guided activity should be presented and how the loop between what a user reports and what the plan does next should be closed, are not answered by the timeline discussion above.

The micro-care screen

Comment 9: the screen the aesthetic chapter was written forEverything argued under Aesthetic grounding is defended in the abstract until it names a screen. This paragraph is where restraint, relevance and the domain palette stop being principles and become five cards. Check the count and durations against the build before submission; they are cited as fact.

The micro-care screen offers five short guided activities (two breathing patterns, two movement sequences, and an eye-rest routine) of one to four minutes each. One is marked as suggested, selected from the current shift phase and from events already logged rather than from a fixed order. This applies the relevance factor directly: a menu identical on every night would be read as generic advice and disregarded, while a menu whose first recommendation moves with the user's reported state is visibly addressed to them (Goli-Cruz, 2023b). The remaining four stay visible and selectable, so the suggestion narrows the choice without removing it, a distinction that matters for a population whose autonomy at work is already low, as the Demand-Control model reviewed earlier in this chapter would predict.

Comment 10: declaring the gap before a panelist finds itThe aesthetic section argues for audio and the content-architecture section lists several media. The build has neither audio nor video. Saying so here, in the section that describes the screen, costs two sentences; being asked about it in a defense costs the credibility of both earlier sections.

Each activity is played through a single guided view carrying an interval countdown, the name of the current step, a pause control, and a progress indicator, with the activity's intervention domain supplying the accent color and icon. Guidance is delivered as timed visual instruction: the audio variant discussed under Aesthetic grounding is specified but not implemented in the build assessed here, and the movement sequences are rendered as timed text rather than as video. This is a limitation of the prototype rather than of the design, since the content model described above admits both without restructuring, and it is recorded so that the scope of what has actually been assessed is not overstated.

The reflection screen

Comment 11: why two logging interfaces is a finding, not a redundancyA reviewer seeing thirteen event types after Table 1 promises five will read it as an inconsistency. Framed as two interfaces answering different questions under different conditions, the same fact becomes evidence that the interruption cost of in-shift logging was designed around rather than ignored.

The prototype separates logging into two interfaces on the grounds that they answer different questions under different conditions. During a shift the user is interrupted and the question is what is happening now, so the in-shift interface is reduced to the five events that alter the remainder of the plan, each returning a single sentence of consequence. After the shift the user is not interrupted and the question is what happened, so the reflection screen exposes the full set of thirteen event types, allows a time to be stated or corrected, and permits any entry to be amended or removed. Clarity, in Goli-Cruz's (2023b) sense of a message constrained by the time and space available to receive it, is therefore a property of the moment rather than of the application, and the two interfaces implement two different answers to it.

The screen returns two derived readings rather than a bare list of entries: a summary of the night's pattern read from the logs, and an explicit statement of which plan items changed as a consequence of them. This is the relevance factor applied to feedback rather than to content. A user who cannot see that a logged event altered the plan has no evidence that logging is worth the effort, and the study treats that visibility as a functional requirement rather than as reassurance.

A closing set of seven fixed-choice questions records the user's own account of the night: sleep duration, restedness, the period of greatest sleepiness, the effect of caffeine, completion of movement breaks, whether rest was taken, and what the plan should change next shift. Only the last of these is permitted to alter the stored profile. The remainder inform the following plan without overwriting the user's stated characteristics, on the reasoning that an answer about what the plan should do next is a preference, while an answer about how rested the user feels is an observation of one night; treating the second as the first would allow a single bad shift to rewrite a standing profile. The design principle at work is the one stated in Chapter I under scope, that the planner adjusts timing rather than diagnosing the user, expressed as a constraint on which inputs are allowed to write.

Software Development Life Cycle

Comment 12: answering the question in its own vocabularyChapter III already cited Domínguez (2017) for the research stance. That does not answer "what SDLC did you follow?", which is how the question will actually be asked in a Multimedia Studies defense. This paragraph answers it in the panel's terms.

The prototype was developed under the Prototype Model of the software development life cycle. This model "focuses on developing a prototype version of the project" and "is applicable if the customer does not have yet specific details about the software they want to have," proceeding by presenting an early build for feedback and applying "necessary changes… based on clients' comments and suggestions"; it is characterized as iterative and as adopting "a trial-and-error method between the client and the developer" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023a).

Comment 13: justify by exclusionNaming the chosen model is weak on its own. Naming the rejected model and quoting its stated disadvantages shows the choice was made rather than defaulted. Two sentences, noticeably more credible.

The model was selected on the grounds the same source gives for model selection generally: "finding the right one greatly depends on the circumstances and factors including but not limited to the scope of the project, size of the team, budget, and client preferences" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023a). Three circumstances of this study favor the Prototype Model. First, its requirements were derived from literature rather than elicited from a client, so no fixed specification existed at the outset. Second, it was developed by a single researcher, for whom the coordination overhead that justifies heavier models does not arise. Third, the Waterfall Model was rejected because its documented disadvantages, that "output is delivered late, severe errors were not detected early" and that it "does not support iterative development" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023a), are precisely the risks a literature-derived prototype must avoid, since a timing rule misread from a study should surface during construction rather than after it.

Comment 14: two disciplines, one conclusionEngineering and design arrive at iteration independently. For a degree that sits between the two, showing the convergence is stronger than either citation alone, and it demonstrates the program's own integration, which is what a capstone is meant to show.

The same iterative commitment is independently supported from the design side, where graphic design is described as "an iterative process" in which "before a graphic design is approved, produced, and used, revisions after revisions may happen," and where proceeding directly to production is discouraged "so as not to waste human and non-human resources" (Goli-Cruz, 2023b).

Requirements Specification

Comment 15: the cheapest section to writeThe requirements already exist implicitly in the prototype's code. This section documents them rather than inventing them, which is why it is worth including: high credibility, low effort. It also converts the artifact from "a thing that was made" into "a system with a specification."

Requirements were specified in two classes. Functional requirements describe "what the system should do, what functionalities and features are, what are the actions to be performed," while non-functional requirements, also termed quality attributes, describe "the general properties of the system aside from the functionalities" and "provide directions and restrictions to the overall system architecture" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023b). Comment 16: a gap the reference work exposedThe original draft stated that no sensitive data is collected. That is true of the research and false of the application. Applying Module 3's quality attributes surfaced the inconsistency. Naming it is not a weakness in the paper; leaving it for the panel to find would be.

Security warrants specific comment. The prototype records sleep duration, fatigue reports, and caffeine intake, which constitute health information about an identifiable individual even though the study itself collects no data from end-users. The scope of the study excludes deployment, but the specification records local-only storage as a requirement so that any subsequent deployment inherits the constraint rather than reconsidering it.

Software Design

Comment 17: a quotable justification for module count"Why four modules and not one, or twelve?" is a fair question with no obvious answer. This quote gives one: complexity is minimized across modules and their integration, not within modules alone.

Design proceeded across the four aspects identified in the reference framework, where design is understood as the stage that "link[s] the requirement analysis phase to the actual system implementation" and where, if requirements analysis "answers the question 'what', software design answers 'how'" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023c). Data design specifies the plan item structure described in Chapter II. Architectural design partitions the system into four modules (time arithmetic, planning rules, statistics, and presentation) on the principle that "overall complexity is the sum of the complexity of each module plus the complexity of the actual integration," and that "complexity represents cost" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023c).

Procedural design is expressed as the rule set that transforms a user profile and event log into a timed plan. The rule set exhibits both patterns the framework describes: it is transform-centered, in that "the input data is then transformed into an output data," and transaction-centered, in that "the input data is evaluated and alternative paths will be selected based on the result of the evaluation" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023c). The latter accounts for the conditional adjustments through which a logged skipped break, failed nap, or late caffeine dose alters subsequent recommendations. Interface design is treated in Chapter II.

Accessibility as a Design Requirement

Accessibility is treated in this study as a functional requirement rather than an optional enhancement, following the position that "it is your responsibility to ensure that the digital products you produce are accessible to people of all abilities" (Esteves, 2025). The conventional justification for accessible design rests on permanent disability. This study advances an additional justification specific to its population.

Comment 18: the strongest argument availableThis connects Chapter II's own cited evidence (Wickwire) to a discipline the thesis had not yet used. It reframes accessibility from a compliance chapter into a restatement of the study's central problem, which is both true and unusually persuasive. If one paragraph from this integration survives, it should be this one.

Night-shift workers exhibit measurable declines in attention, working memory, and response inhibition that accumulate across consecutive shifts (Wickwire et al., 2021). An interface operated under those conditions faces constraints functionally similar to those that accessibility standards are constructed to accommodate, differing in origin, situational rather than permanent, but not in kind. Conformance to WCAG 2.2 at Level AA (World Wide Web Consortium [W3C], 2023) is therefore adopted as a design requirement responding directly to the cognitive profile documented in Chapter II, and not as an ancillary compliance activity.

Comment 19: the vocabulary the previous paragraph is missingThe situational argument is strong but leaves an opening: "so you are calling tiredness a disability?" This paragraph closes it by borrowing the course's own three-way distinction, which lets the claim be made about design rather than about the user's status.

The course from which this requirement is drawn distinguishes universal design, inclusive design, and accessible design, and treats meeting the needs of users whose capacities vary, including ageing users, as continuous with meeting the needs of users with disabilities (Esteves, 2025). That continuity is what allows the preceding argument to be made without overstatement. The study does not claim that fatigue constitutes a disability; it claims that a design accommodating varied and fluctuating capacity serves both populations, and that the techniques are the same in either case.

Comment 20: naming the procedure, not just the standardCiting WCAG alone says which rules were used. Naming the preliminary review, the tooling and the WCAG-EM report format says the audit was conducted a particular way, which is the difference between a procedure a reader can repeat and a claim a reader must accept. The WCAG-EM report tool output belongs in an appendix once the audit is re-run on the finished build.

Conformance is organized by the four principles under which the success criteria are grouped: perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust (W3C, 2023). The procedure follows the sequence taught for accessibility auditing, namely a preliminary review of the obvious barriers, followed by a structured conformance evaluation conducted under the Website Accessibility Conformance Evaluation Methodology and recorded in its report format (Esteves, 2025; W3C, 2014). The evaluation is restricted to the prototype's implemented screens, and its scope statement records that restriction explicitly, since a conformance claim covering screens that do not yet exist would be void.

Comment 21: report the failuresAn audit that finds nothing reads as an audit that was not run. Three findings, one closed by existing redundancy and two left open with measured values, are far more credible than a bare claim of conformance, and cost nothing, since all three are real. The earlier draft of this paragraph said the contrast issue "was adjusted." It was not, and a panelist with a color picker would have found that out.

Three findings from the initial audit are recorded here rather than silently corrected. First, the lowest-emphasis text color in each theme falls below the 4.5:1 ratio required at Level AA for body text, measuring 2.51:1 in the light theme and 3.17:1 in the dark theme against their respective surfaces; the value is used for de-emphasized supporting text, and remediation is outstanding rather than complete. Second, the prototype distinguishes eight intervention domains by hue, a scheme under which two domain colors converge under deuteranopia; because each domain also carries a distinct icon, color is a redundant rather than a sole channel, and this finding is closed. Third, the micro-care screen animates a scaling indicator across each timed interval. A pause control is provided, which satisfies the criterion governing moving content, but the prototype does not honor the operating system's reduced-motion preference, and that finding is also outstanding.

Comment 22: the cheapest accessibility argument in the thesisUsing a native control instead of rebuilding one is a decision that was made for other reasons and turns out to be the accessible choice. Claiming it is honest and free. It also demonstrates the robustness principle concretely, which the four-principles paragraph otherwise leaves abstract.

One implementation decision is recorded as satisfying the requirement rather than failing it. The selection controls on the reflection screen are native platform controls associated with visible labels, rather than custom widgets reconstructed from generic elements. Native controls carry keyboard operation, focus behavior, and assistive-technology semantics without additional authoring, which is the robustness principle applied at the level of implementation rather than of visual design, and which is the reason the reflection screen was rebuilt around them.

Verification and Validation

Comment 23: the single most useful correctionThe original Chapter III used "validation" for everything. Splitting the terms costs one paragraph and reorganizes the whole section: the test suite and traceability check become verification, the expert review becomes validation. Both were already being done; only the vocabulary was missing.

This study distinguishes verification from validation following Boehm, where verification asks "are we building the product right?" and validation asks "are we building the right product?", the latter being necessary because "there are times when the specification requirements do not represent the real needs of the customers" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023d). The distinction reorganizes the study's assessment activities into two groups.

Comment 24: the traceability promise, made operationalChapter III already promised a traceability check. Storing citation keys on each plan item turns that promise from an appendix maintained by hand into something the system can report on. It is also the clearest evidence that the circadian literature genuinely governs the artifact.

Verification comprises two procedures. Automated unit tests exercise the scheduling, statistics, and time-arithmetic modules, unit testing being the strategy that "focuses on checking if the unit of code (smallest testable part) is fit to use" and is "done by the developer itself" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023d). The traceability check confirms that each planning rule corresponds to at least one supporting study, and is performed against citation identifiers recorded on each plan item alongside its user-facing rationale, so that the check is executed against the running system rather than against separately maintained documentation.

Comment 25: legitimizing the method most open to challengeExpert review instead of user testing is the study's most contestable choice. This citation establishes heuristic evaluation as taught disciplinary practice, making it a deliberate selection rather than a substitute for testing that could not be arranged.

Validation comprises expert heuristic review and scenario-based testing. Heuristic evaluation is adopted as an established interface assessment method in multimedia practice, taught alongside usability testing as a means to "evaluate a user interface using heuristics and usability tests" and to "create user interfaces based on heuristic and human-centered principles" (Clariño, 2024). Its use here is a methodological selection appropriate to a prototype whose scope excludes end-user trials, and is not offered as equivalent to them.

Comment 26: an instrument with provenanceChapter III requires a "structured evaluation guide." Adapting a rubric that already exists and citing it is stronger than composing one at submission time, and it takes an afternoon rather than a week.

The evaluation instrument adapts the criteria of an established design-analysis rubric, namely context and purpose, enumeration of visual elements and design principles applied or violated, assessment of effectiveness against intended purpose, and improvement recommendations (Goli-Cruz, 2023c). Numerical scoring is removed in favor of qualitative judgment, consistent with the instrument requirements stated earlier in this chapter.

Limitations of the Assessment Approach

Comment 27: the limitation that also justifies the testsThis quote does two jobs: it bounds the claims that can be made, and it explains why a student prototype has an automated test suite at all. Health-adjacent software warrants a higher confidence level; that is the argument, and it comes from the source.

Two limitations follow from the approach above. First, testing establishes the presence of defects but not their absence: it "discloses the presence of bugs, but not their absence," so that "we can only address the problems that we have encountered but we cannot do something about the ones we did not discover" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023d). The point carries additional weight for this study, since "systems concerning health should have a higher level of confidence than that of supply inventory" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023d), and a prototype validated by expert review alone therefore retains residual risk that the study does not claim to eliminate.

Second, acceptance testing, the strategy that verifies "if the requirements of the clients are all satisfied" and is "usually carried out by the end-users" (University of the Philippines Open University, 2023d), falls outside the declared scope of this study. Accordingly, no claim is advanced regarding the prototype's effectiveness in improving health outcomes or altering behavior, consistent with the scope and limitations set out in Chapter I.
